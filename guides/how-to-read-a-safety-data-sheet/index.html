How to Read a Safety Data Sheet (SDS): All 16 Sections Explained

On this page
  1. The format: 16 sections in a fixed order
  2. Section 2: reading the hazard summary
  3. The sections a field worker uses most
  4. Limits of an SDS

Every hazardous chemical sold in the United States ships with a safety data sheet. OSHA's Hazard Communication Standard (29 CFR 1910.1200) requires manufacturers and importers to write one, and employers to keep it readily accessible to workers on every shift. On a hazardous waste site or during a spill, the SDS is often the fastest way to learn what a chemical can do to you and how to protect yourself.

The format: 16 sections in a fixed order

Since HazCom was aligned with the Globally Harmonized System (GHS), every SDS uses the same 16 headings in the same order (Appendix D to 1910.1200). Once you know where things are, you can find answers in seconds.

#SectionLook here for
1IdentificationProduct name, recommended use, supplier, emergency phone number
2Hazard(s) identificationHazard class, signal word, pictograms, hazard and precautionary statements
3Composition / ingredientsChemical names, CAS numbers, concentrations
4First-aid measuresWhat to do for inhalation, skin, eye and ingestion exposures; key symptoms
5Fire-fighting measuresSuitable extinguishing media, hazardous combustion products, firefighter PPE
6Accidental release measuresSpill PPE, containment and cleanup methods
7Handling and storageSafe handling, incompatible materials, storage conditions
8Exposure controls / personal protectionOSHA PELs, ACGIH TLVs, engineering controls, gloves, respirators
9Physical and chemical propertiesFlash point, vapor pressure, vapor density, pH, solubility, explosive limits
10Stability and reactivityConditions and materials to avoid, hazardous decomposition products
11Toxicological informationRoutes of exposure, symptoms, acute and chronic effects, carcinogenicity
12-15Ecological, disposal, transport, regulatoryRequired on the sheet, but OSHA does not enforce their content because other agencies regulate those areas
16Other informationDate of preparation or last revision

Section 2: reading the hazard summary

Section 2 is the one-glance summary, and it matches the container label.

  • Signal word. "Danger" is used for the more severe hazards and "Warning" for the less severe. Only one appears.
  • Pictograms. Red-bordered diamonds: flame, flame over circle (oxidizer), exploding bomb, gas cylinder, corrosion, skull and crossbones, exclamation mark, health hazard (the silhouette with a starburst) and environment. OSHA does not require the environment pictogram.
  • Hazard statements describe the hazard ("Causes severe skin burns and eye damage"). Precautionary statements tell you what to do ("Wear protective gloves").

The sections a field worker uses most

When you are deciding how to work safely, go straight to these:

  1. Section 8 for exposure limits and the PPE the manufacturer recommends. Compare the limits with your air monitoring readings.
  2. Section 9 for flash point (fire risk), vapor density (above 1 means vapors sink into low areas) and the lower and upper explosive limits.
  3. Section 10 for incompatibles, so you don't store or bulk a waste with something it reacts with.
  4. Section 4 before work starts, so first aid isn't a surprise.
  5. Section 6 if something spills.

Limits of an SDS

  • It describes the product as sold. Waste mixtures, aged drums and reaction products may behave differently.
  • Glove advice is often general ("chemical-resistant gloves"). Check a glove manufacturer's permeation chart for the actual chemical.
  • Check the revision date in Section 16 and ask for the current sheet if it looks old.
  • On an unknown-waste site there may be no SDS at all, which is why site characterization and air monitoring come first.

Frequently asked questions

How many sections are on a safety data sheet?

Sixteen, always in the same order, as required by OSHA 29 CFR 1910.1200 Appendix D.

Which SDS section lists the PPE to wear?

Section 8, Exposure controls/personal protection, which also lists exposure limits such as OSHA PELs and ACGIH TLVs.

What is the difference between the signal words Danger and Warning?

Danger is used for more severe hazards and Warning for less severe hazards. Only one signal word appears on a label or SDS.
