Toxicology Basics for Hazmat Workers: Dose, Routes and Health Effects

  3. How chemicals get in
  4. Local and systemic effects
  5. Special kinds of toxicity

Toxicology is the study of how chemicals harm living things. You don't need a degree to use its basic ideas, and they explain most of the safety rules you follow on a hazardous waste site.

The dose makes the poison

Almost anything is harmful at a high enough dose, and many dangerous chemicals are harmless at a low enough one. Dose depends on concentration (how much is in the air, water or soil), duration (how long you're exposed) and frequency (how often). Controls such as ventilation, PPE, work-rest cycles and staying upwind all work by reducing one of those three.

A dose-response curve shows how the effect grows as the dose increases. For many effects there's a threshold below which no harm is seen. For some carcinogens, regulators assume there is no completely safe threshold, which is why exposures are kept as low as reasonably possible.

LD50 and LC50

  • LD50 (lethal dose 50%): the dose, usually in milligrams per kilogram of body weight, that killed half of the test animals. A smaller LD50 means a more toxic chemical.
  • LC50 (lethal concentration 50%): the same idea for an airborne concentration over a set time.

These numbers compare the acute toxicity of chemicals. They say nothing about long-term effects such as cancer, and they are not "safe" levels for people. You'll find them in Section 11 of the safety data sheet.

How chemicals get in

  • Inhalation: gases, vapors, mists, fumes and dusts. The lungs have a huge surface area and pass chemicals into the blood quickly.
  • Skin and eye contact: corrosives burn on contact, and many solvents and pesticides pass through intact skin. A "skin" notation on an exposure limit flags this.
  • Ingestion: usually hand-to-mouth from eating, drinking or smoking without washing.
  • Injection: punctures from contaminated sharps, glass or metal.

Local and systemic effects

A local effect happens where the chemical touches the body: an acid burn, eye irritation, a cough from an irritant gas. A systemic effect happens after the chemical is absorbed and carried elsewhere. The organ a chemical mainly damages is its target organ.

Special kinds of toxicity

  • Carcinogens can cause cancer, often years after exposure.
  • Mutagens damage genetic material.
  • Reproductive toxins and teratogens affect fertility or harm a developing fetus.
  • Sensitizers cause an allergic reaction that gets worse with each exposure. Once sensitized, a worker may react to tiny amounts.
  • Asphyxiants: simple asphyxiants such as nitrogen or argon displace oxygen; chemical asphyxiants such as carbon monoxide or hydrogen cyanide stop the body using it.

When chemicals combine

  • Additive: the combined effect equals the sum of the parts.
  • Synergistic: the combined effect is greater than the sum. Smoking and asbestos exposure together raise lung cancer risk far more than either alone.
  • Potentiation: a chemical that's harmless alone makes another one more toxic.

Hazardous waste sites usually involve mixtures, which is one reason HAZWOPER leans on conservative controls and medical surveillance. Check what you've learned with the toxicology quiz.

Frequently asked questions

What does LD50 mean?

The dose of a chemical that killed 50% of test animals, usually given in milligrams per kilogram of body weight. A lower LD50 means higher acute toxicity.

What is a target organ?

The organ a chemical mainly damages after it is absorbed, such as the liver, kidneys, nervous system, blood or lungs.

What is a synergistic effect?

When two exposures together cause a greater effect than the sum of each alone, such as smoking combined with asbestos exposure.
