# Heat Stress in Chemical Protective Clothing: Signs, Prevention and Monitoring

Free lesson · 3 min read · Updated 2026-10-30

On many hazardous waste sites, heat hurts more people than chemicals do. Chemical protective clothing is designed to keep liquids and vapors out, which also stops sweat from evaporating, and evaporation is the body's main way of cooling off. Workers in Level A or B can overheat even on a mild day.

## Heat illnesses from mild to deadly

| Illness | Signs | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Heat rash | Red, itchy bumps where clothing traps sweat | Keep skin dry, rest in a cool place |
| Heat cramps | Painful muscle spasms during or after work | Rest, drink water or an electrolyte drink |
| Heat syncope | Fainting or dizziness when standing for long periods or standing up suddenly | Sit or lie down in the shade, drink fluids |
| Heat exhaustion | Heavy sweating, weakness, headache, nausea, cool clammy skin, fast pulse | Stop work, move to shade, remove PPE after decon, cool down, drink. Get medical help if no better within an hour |
| Heat stroke | Confusion, slurred speech, collapse, seizures, very high body temperature; skin may be hot and dry or still sweating | **Medical emergency.** Call 911, cool the person immediately with water, ice or wet cloths, and stay with them |

Confusion is the warning sign that separates heat stroke from heat exhaustion. Don't wait for a worker to stop sweating before calling for help.

## Who is most at risk

- New workers and anyone returning from a week or more away, who aren't acclimatized
- Workers in fully encapsulating suits or heavy turnout gear
- People who are dehydrated, ill, or taking certain medications
- Anyone doing heavy work such as shoveling, drum handling or carrying equipment

## Prevention on site

- **Acclimatize** new workers by building up their time in heat and PPE gradually over the first one to two weeks.
- **Work-rest cycles**: plan shorter work periods in heavier PPE and hotter conditions, with rest in a shaded, cool area.
- **Hydrate** before, during and after work. A common recommendation is about one cup of water every 15 to 20 minutes during work in the heat, rather than large amounts at once.
- **Schedule** heavy work for the cooler parts of the day where possible.
- **Cooling gear**: cooling vests with ice or phase-change packs, cooled air in supplied-air systems, and shaded rest areas with fans or misters.
- **Buddy system**: partners watch each other for early signs.

## Physiological monitoring

When workers wear impermeable clothing in warm conditions, guidance from NIOSH, OSHA, the Coast Guard and EPA recommends monitoring them during rest breaks. Common methods:

- **Heart rate** at the start of the rest period. If it is above 110 beats per minute, shorten the next work cycle by a third and keep the rest period the same.
- **Body temperature** (oral or ear) at the end of the work period. If it is above 99.6°F (37.6°C), shorten the next work cycle by a third. A worker shouldn't go back into impermeable clothing with a temperature above 100.6°F (38.1°C).
- **Body weight** before and after the shift, to check fluids are being replaced.

Your site safety and health plan sets the actual work-rest schedule and monitoring triggers.

## State heat rules

Some states have their own heat illness standards, including California (outdoor and indoor rules in Title 8), Washington, Oregon, Colorado (agriculture), Minnesota (indoor) and Maryland. If you work in one of these states, follow the state rule as well as your site plan. Check yourself with the [heat stress quiz](https://www.hazwoperosha.org/quizzes/heat-stress-quiz/).

## Frequently asked questions

### Why does chemical protective clothing cause heat stress?

It stops sweat from evaporating, which is the body's main way of cooling down, so body heat builds up even in mild weather.

### What is the difference between heat exhaustion and heat stroke?

Heat exhaustion causes heavy sweating, weakness and nausea. Heat stroke adds confusion, collapse or seizures and a very high body temperature, and is a medical emergency requiring 911 and immediate cooling.

### How is heat stress monitored for workers in protective suits?

Common methods are checking heart rate at the start of rest, body temperature at the end of work, and body weight before and after the shift, then adjusting work-rest cycles.
