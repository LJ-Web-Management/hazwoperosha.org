Hazardous Chemical Spill Response: Step by Step

On this page
  1. Incidental release or emergency?
  2. The steps
  3. What goes in a spill kit
  4. Reporting requirements

What you should do about a chemical spill depends first on one question: is it an incidental release or an emergency? The answer decides whether you clean it up or get away and call for help.

Incidental release or emergency?

OSHA treats a release as incidental when the substance can be absorbed, neutralized or otherwise controlled at the time of release by employees in the immediate work area or by maintenance staff, and it poses no significant safety or health hazard. Cleaning up incidental spills isn't HAZWOPER emergency response, though workers still need hazard communication training and the right PPE.

It's an emergency if any of these apply:

  • The release is large, spreading, or you can't tell how big it is
  • You don't know what the material is
  • People are injured or showing symptoms
  • There's fire, explosion risk, or a toxic vapor cloud
  • It needs more help or equipment than people in the area have
  • It's reaching drains, waterways or soil

Only workers trained under HAZWOPER paragraph (q) may respond to an emergency release.

The steps

  1. Stop and assess from a distance. Look for placards, labels, vapors, odors, fire and injured people. Don't touch, walk through or breathe the material.
  2. Protect yourself and others. Move upwind and uphill. Warn people nearby.
  3. Isolate the area. Keep people out. For transportation incidents, use the isolation distance in the Emergency Response Guidebook.
  4. Notify. Follow your emergency response plan: supervisor, emergency coordinator, 911 or your facility's emergency number.
  5. Identify the material from labels, shipping papers or the safety data sheet.
  6. Contain, if trained. Operations-level responders take defensive action: diking, damming, diverting, covering drains, absorbing.
  7. Stop the leak, if trained. Plugging and patching is technician-level work.
  8. Clean up and decontaminate. Package waste in labeled containers, decontaminate people and equipment, and restock the spill kit.
  9. Report and review. Make required reports and learn from what happened.

What goes in a spill kit

  • Absorbents matched to the chemicals on site: universal, oil-only or hazmat/chemical (for acids and bases)
  • Socks or booms for diking, pads and loose absorbent
  • Drain covers or plugs
  • Neutralizers for acids or bases, where appropriate
  • PPE: chemical-resistant gloves, goggles, face shield, suits and boot covers
  • Non-sparking scoop and scraper, disposal bags, ties and labels, and an overpack container

Reporting requirements

Releases of a hazardous substance at or above its reportable quantity within 24 hours must be reported right away to the National Response Center at 1-800-424-8802 under CERCLA. EPCRA may also require notice to state and local emergency planning bodies, oil spills into water have their own reporting rules, and many states set lower thresholds. Your emergency response plan should list who makes these calls. Check your knowledge with the field safety quiz.

Frequently asked questions

What is an incidental chemical release?

A release that can be absorbed, neutralized or otherwise controlled at the time by employees in the immediate area or by maintenance staff, and that poses no significant safety or health hazard. It is not HAZWOPER emergency response.

What number do you call to report a hazardous substance release?

The National Response Center at 1-800-424-8802, for releases at or above the CERCLA reportable quantity. Follow your emergency plan and any state rules as well.

Who can respond to an emergency chemical release?

Only workers trained under HAZWOPER paragraph (q) to the level of the duties they will perform.
